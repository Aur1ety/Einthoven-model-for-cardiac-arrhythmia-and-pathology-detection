Einthoven Project Analysis

Einthoven: Physically Grounded Deep Learning for ECG Diagnosis

What is this project?

The one-line version: an ECG machine gives you 12 separate traces. Physics says those 12 traces are just 2D shadows of one 3D electrical vector beating inside the heart. This project tests whether giving a neural network that reconstructed 3D vector (the vectorcardiogram, or VCG) on top of the 12 raw traces makes it better at diagnosing heart conditions.

The hypothesis: modern deep learning models treat the 12 leads as a flat pile of 12 signals and learn the relationships between them themselves. But the relationships are actually known: they are fixed linear equations published by Kors et al. in 1990, which rebuild the 3D cardiac vector from leads I, II, V1 to V6. If we hand the model the physics directly as 3 extra channels, maybe it learns faster and diagnoses better. The honest question is whether it already figures all of that out on its own.

Short answer (spoiler): under our exact protocol, the 3 physics channels added no measurable improvement. That is a real, defensible negative result, and the project still produced two genuinely useful side findings (a quality-control audit and a cross-dataset generalization study) that stand on their own.

The data

Primary dataset: PTB-XL (Wagner et al. 2020, Scientific Data)

  • 21,799 real 12-lead ECG recordings from 18,869 patients, 100 Hz, 10-second windows
  • Every record has cardiologist-verified labels in the SCP-ECG coding system
  • Labels are grouped into 5 diagnostic superclasses:
    • NORM (normal), MI (heart attack history), STTC (ST/T changes), CD (conduction disturbances), HYP (hypertrophy)
  • 411 records are excluded from the classification task because their SCP labels map to none of the 5 diagnostic superclasses, leaving the 21,388-record standard PTB-XL superdiagnostic subset (this is a label-coverage filter, not a pacemaker filter; 291 records carry a pacemaker annotation and most, but not all, fall inside these 411)
  • Official split used throughout, exactly as the dataset paper prescribes: folds 1 to 8 for training, fold 9 for validation, fold 10 for testing. Splits are patient-disjoint (the same patient never appears in both train and test), which prevents the model from memorizing a person instead of learning a pattern

Secondary dataset (generalization test): CPSC2018 (PhysioNet, CC BY 4.0)

  • 6,877 real ECGs recorded in China, 12 leads, 500 Hz
  • 9 native classes: Normal, AF, I-AVB, LBBB, RBBB, PAC, PVC, STD, STE
  • Used to test whether a model trained on German patients also works on Chinese patients. Zero training on this data: the PTB-XL-trained model was only run, never fine-tuned

The method

Step 1. Reproduce the official baselines. Using the official benchmarking code (helme/ecg_ptbxl_benchmarking, Strodthoff et al. 2020, arXiv:2004.13701), two 12-channel models were trained on the exact official split:

ModelTest AUC
resnet1d_wang0.9293
inception1d0.9219

These match the published benchmark numbers, confirming the pipeline is correct before any new idea is tested.

Step 2. The VCG transform. The Kors 1990 regression (Kors et al. 1990, European Heart Journal) computes 3 channels (X, Y, Z = the 3D cardiac vector) from 8 of the 12 leads, using 24 published coefficients. All 24 coefficients were independently cross-verified against a second open-access source (Jaros et al. 2019, Sensors, Table 2) and agree exactly. The VCG model simply gets those 3 extra channels alongside the 12 raw leads (15 channels total).

Step 3. The controlled A/B test (Phase B). Identical architecture, hyperparameters, random seed, and train/val/test split. Only the input channels differ: 12 (control) vs 15 (VCG-augmented).

The results

Finding 1: Phase B null result (the main test)

Paired difference (VCG minus control) = +0.00055, 95% CI [−0.00216, +0.00305]. The CI crosses zero: no statistically significant difference. The physics channels add no measurable diagnostic value on top of the raw leads under this architecture and protocol. The neural network already extracts everything the VCG gives it.

Finding 2: the Einthoven-consistency audit (standalone, citable)

Because the 12 limb leads obey exact arithmetic identities (Einthoven's triangle: II = I + III, and similar rules for the augmented leads), every record can be checked for internal consistency. We ran this check on all 21,799 PTB-XL records:

  • 99.74% of records are consistent to within 1 to 1.5 microvolts (the quantization level of the measurement hardware)
  • Only 57 records (0.26%) are flagged, and all of them are heavy-tailed recording artifacts (motion, electrode pop-off, lead disconnect), not arithmetic errors
  • PTB-XL's own built-in "noisy" annotations do not predict the audit flag (0.20% of annotated-noisy records flagged vs 0.28% of unannotated records), so the two systems catch different failure modes

This is a standalone quality-control result for anyone using PTB-XL: the audit dataset and the verified transform coefficients are released in the repository.

Finding 3: do the residuals carry diagnostic info? (Spec 05)

Follow-up question: the consistency checks leave a small residual (how far a record deviates from the exact identity). Could that residual differ by diagnosis? Tested on 21,508 records with proper multiple-comparison correction (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR, alpha 0.05):

  • 4 Kruskal-Wallis tests (one per residual type): 3 survive correction, but the largest effect size is eta-squared = 0.0039, far below the 0.01 "small effect" threshold
  • 20 Mann-Whitney tests (4 residual types x 5 classes): 8 survive correction, but the largest Cliff's delta is 0.044, well below the 0.147 "negligible to small" boundary

Conclusion: statistically significant, practically negligible. A dataset this big detects tiny distributional shifts that carry no useful diagnostic information. The residuals do not help distinguish patients.

Finding 4: cross-dataset generalization to CPSC2018 (Spec 06)

The PTB-XL-trained resnet1d_wang model was run (zero training) on 4,746 usable CPSC2018 records. Important limitation, stated up front: CPSC2018 contains zero MI and zero HYP records, so this only tests 3 of the 5 superclasses (NORM, CD, STTC).

Full set (STD + STE as STTC)4,7460.7765[0.7679, 0.7833]

Compared to the in-distribution PTB-XL AUC of 0.9293, performance drops substantially, and the drop is not uniform:

  • NORM generalizes best (AUC 0.849 to 0.878): a normal ECG looks nearly the same on any patient, anywhere in the world
  • CD generalizes moderately (AUC 0.813 to 0.819): conduction blocks produce distinctive, anatomy-driven waveforms (wide QRS, axis deviation) that transfer across populations
  • STTC generalizes worst (AUC 0.667 with STE, 0.729 without): ST/T changes are subtle, low-level morphology deviations highly sensitive to electrode placement, baseline wander, and label conventions

The three-variant design isolates the STE question: removing STE raises AUC by +0.025, but removing the remaining ST-T records gives another +0.046. So the STE label-mapping decision explains roughly one-third of the STTC gap; the other two-thirds is inherent: STTC as a class simply does not transfer well from PTB-XL to CPSC2018.

What the results mean

Three honest takeaways:

  1. The main hypothesis did not survive the test. For resnet1d_wang on PTB-XL, 3 known-physics VCG channels are redundant: the model learns the same information from the raw leads. This is a negative result reported as a negative result, not papered over.
  2. The negative result is informative, not worthless. It says that for this architecture family and this task, physics-grounded inputs do not beat learned inputs. The value of VCG-based inputs remains open for architectures that cannot learn lead relationships as well (shallower models, smaller datasets, different tasks).
  3. The side findings are real contributions. (a) A ready-to-use consistency audit for PTB-XL with verified Kors coefficients, (b) a corrected, FDR-controlled analysis of residual-diagnosis dependence, (c) a zero-training cross-dataset generalization study showing which superclasses transfer across populations and which do not.
